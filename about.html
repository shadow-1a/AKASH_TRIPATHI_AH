A city older than its maps

Prayagraj, known for most of modern history as Allahabad, sits in the south-east of Uttar Pradesh at the point where the Ganga and the Yamuna meet. For Hindus it is Tirtharaj, the "king of pilgrimage sites", and few places in India carry as much religious weight. Yet the city is far more than a pilgrimage stop. It is a place where ancient scriptures, Mughal forts, colonial courts, nationalist movements and modern universities all share the same few kilometres of river bank.

Visitors often arrive for a single reason, usually a holy dip at the Sangam, and leave surprised by how much else there is to see. Within a short ride you can watch a boat glide across the muddy-green line where two rivers mingle, walk the outer walls of a sixteenth century fort, stand in the house where a future prime minister grew up, and eat hot kachori at a street stall that has been open for generations. This page gives you the background to make the most of all of it.

Name and history

The name Prayag, meaning "place of sacrifice" or "confluence", appears in the Puranas and the epics. Tradition holds that Brahma performed a great sacrifice here, which is why the site came to be called Prayag. The Ramayana places the ashram of Sage Bharadwaj nearby, where Rama, Sita and Lakshmana are said to have rested on their way into exile. The Mahabharata and several Puranas praise a bath at the confluence as equal to countless ritual offerings.

Written history is just as rich. The region was known as Kaushambi's neighbour in the age of the early kingdoms, and the Ashoka Pillar now standing inside the fort dates to the third century BCE. Later rulers added inscriptions to the same pillar, including a famous praise-poem for the Gupta emperor Samudragupta. The Chinese pilgrim Hiuen Tsang visited around the seventh century CE and described a great festival on the sands by the river, a tradition that clearly continued into our time.

In 1583 the Mughal emperor Akbar built the massive fort at the confluence and renamed the city Illahabas, later written Allahabad. Under the Mughals it became an important provincial capital. After the Revolt of 1857 the British made it the seat of the North-Western Provinces, and in 1858 the proclamation transferring power from the East India Company to the Crown was read here. In October 2018 the city was officially renamed Prayagraj, returning to its older name.

The Allahabad Fort stands on the bank at the confluence, built by Akbar in 1583.

The Triveni Sangam

Boats carry pilgrims to the exact meeting point of the rivers.

The Sangam is the heart of Prayagraj. Here the pale, clear Ganga meets the darker, greenish Yamuna, and the two colours run side by side before blending. Tradition adds a third, unseen river, the Saraswati, which is believed to flow beneath the surface and join them. This is why the place is called Triveni, the "three-fold confluence".

Boatmen take visitors out to the exact meeting point, where a shallow platform lets pilgrims bathe. Mornings are the most atmospheric, with mist on the water, priests chanting on the bank and flocks of Siberian cranes visible in the cold months. Evenings bring lamps and aarti at the ghats nearby. Close to the Sangam you will also find the Bade Hanuman Mandir, with its unusual reclining Hanuman, and the fort with its Patalpuri temple and the sacred Akshayavat tree.

Bathing at the Sangam is considered especially meritorious on new moon days, and during festival periods. If you are not comfortable entering the river, you can simply sit on the bank, sprinkle water on your head and enjoy the view, as many local families do.

Kumbh Mela and Magh Mela

Prayagraj is one of four places that host the Kumbh Mela, the others being Haridwar, Ujjain and Nashik. The festival is rooted in the story of the churning of the ocean, when drops of the nectar of immortality fell at these four sites. The Maha Kumbh at Prayagraj comes once every twelve years, with an Ardh (half) Kumbh in between. In the years without a Kumbh, the Magh Mela is held every winter on the river sands, and many families keep a month-long vow of simple living on the bank during that time.

The scale of these gatherings is hard to picture. A temporary city of tents, roads, bridges, hospitals and kitchens rises on the floodplain each time, and the most important bathing days draw crowds counted in many millions. The 2025 Maha Kumbh was widely reported as among the largest human gatherings ever recorded. Whether or not you visit during a mela, you can feel its legacy in the wide embankments, the ghats and the temporary-looking lanes that fill up each winter.

If you plan to come during Kumbh or Magh Mela, book transport and stay well in advance, expect road closures near the river, and carry only light luggage. Our Kumbh Mela page has more detail.

Heart of the freedom struggle

Few Indian cities played as central a role in the independence movement as Allahabad. In 1857 local leaders such as Maulvi Liaquat Ali took part in the uprising here. Later the city became a base for the Indian National Congress. Anand Bhawan and the neighbouring Swaraj Bhawan, homes of the Nehru family, served as meeting places for national leaders and are now museums where you can see letters, photographs and rooms preserved as they were. Jawaharlal Nehru and Indira Gandhi were both born in the city.

The revolutionary Chandrashekhar Azad is remembered here too. In February 1931 he died in a gunfight with police in what was then Alfred Park, and the park is today named after him. A statue and a museum mark the spot, and the large green space is still a favourite for morning walks. These sites together make Prayagraj one of the best places in India to understand the freedom movement through real buildings and objects rather than just textbooks.

Anand Bhawan, the Nehru family home, is now a museum.

Learning, law and literature

The University of Allahabad, founded in 1887, is among the oldest modern universities in India and was once nicknamed the "Oxford of the East". Its red-stone buildings and the Muir College tower are worth a slow walk. The city is also home to the Allahabad High Court, one of the oldest and busiest high courts in the country, and to a famous culture of competitive exam preparation, which fills the lanes around the university with students, bookshops and tea stalls.

Prayagraj has a deep literary tradition as well. Hindi poets Suryakant Tripathi "Nirala", Mahadevi Verma and Sumitranandan Pant lived and wrote here, as did the Urdu poet Firaq Gorakhpuri. Harivansh Rai Bachchan, author of the celebrated poem Madhushala, lived in the city, and his son, the actor Amitabh Bachchan, was born here. Even today you can find small literary gatherings, old publishing houses and second-hand book stalls in the civil lines area.

Culture, people and food

The Allahabadi guava is famous across India.

Daily life in Prayagraj mixes the sacred and the ordinary. Priests known as pandas guide pilgrims at the ghats, each family traditionally served by a particular panda lineage. Hindi and Awadhi-flavoured Hindi are widely spoken, and Urdu poetry still has its fans. Festivals follow the Hindu calendar, with Makar Sankranti, Mauni Amavasya, Basant Panchami, Holi and Diwali the biggest, and the Ramlila and Dussehra processions are loved by the whole city.

The food is hearty and vegetarian-friendly. Breakfast usually means hot kachori with aloo sabzi, followed by jalebi or imarti. Street chaat, such as tamatar chaat and dahi bhalla, is a speciality of the Chowk and Civil Lines markets, and a glass of thick lassi or malai makhan in winter is hard to beat. The city is also known for the Allahabadi guava, often sweet and fragrant, which is in season in the colder months. Our Food page lists good places to try these dishes.

Locals are known for being polite, talkative and happy to give directions or tell you about their city's past. A little Hindi goes a long way, though basic English is understood in hotels and at major sights.

Climate and best time to visit

Winter

October to March. Cool, dry and pleasant, with chilly mornings in December and January. This is the best season for sightseeing and for the Magh Mela.

Summer

April to June. Very hot, often above 40°C. Plan sightseeing for early morning and evening, and carry water and a hat.

Monsoon

July to September. Warm and humid, and the rivers rise. Low ghats, and even parts of the Sangam area, can be under water.

How to reach Prayagraj

By train

Prayagraj Junction and several other stations on the main Delhi to Howrah line make rail the easiest way in. Direct trains run from Delhi, Mathura, Kanpur, Lucknow, Varanasi and Kolkata.

By road

National highways link the city to Varanasi (about 120 km), Lucknow (about 200 km) and Kanpur. State and private buses, including AC sleepers, run daily.

By air

Prayagraj Airport at Bamrauli has flights to major cities. The nearest large international airports are at Varanasi and Lucknow.

Practical tips

  • Dress modestly at temples and ghats, and carry a cloth or scarf for covering your head where needed.
  • Fix the boat fare at the Sangam before you sit, and prefer shared boats during crowded days.
  • Keep valuables safe while bathing, and use the locker facilities at the ghats where available.
  • Auto-rickshaws and e-rickshaws are the common local transport, so confirm the fare in advance or use a ride-hailing app.
  • Some places, such as parts of the fort, may be closed or restricted, so check timings locally before you go.
  • Respect the sacred nature of the rivers. Avoid throwing plastic or other waste into the water.
