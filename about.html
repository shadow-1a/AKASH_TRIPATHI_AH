Overview

Prayagraj, known as Allahabad for more than four centuries, is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in India and one of the holiest. It lies in the south-east of Uttar Pradesh on the Indo-Gangetic plain, at the point where the Ganga and the Yamuna meet. Hindu tradition adds a third river, the Saraswati, said to flow unseen beneath the surface, and calls the meeting point the Triveni Sangam. For pilgrims the Sangam is the centre of the city and the reason for its fame.

Prayagraj is far more than a pilgrimage town, though. Over the centuries it has been a sacred centre described in the Puranas, a royal assembly ground, a Mughal fortress city, a battlefield of 1857, a seat of British provincial government, the home of the Nehru family and a headquarters of India's freedom movement. Today it is also a city of courts, universities, poets, coaching classes and students, with a busy market life and a famous street food scene.

This guide walks through the story of the city from its earliest mentions to the present day, then introduces the places worth visiting and the practical things a traveller should know. You can read it from top to bottom or jump to any section with the links above.

The many names of the city

The oldest name is Prayag. The word is usually explained as "place of sacrifice" or "place of confluence", and it appears in ancient Sanskrit texts. The Puranas describe Prayag as Tirtharaj, the king of all pilgrimage sites, and say that the creator Brahma performed a great sacrifice here. This sacred reputation is much older than any fort, university or railway station in the city.

The Mughal emperor Akbar chose the confluence for a great fortress in the 1570s and gave the settlement around it the name Illahabas, often explained as "abode of God". In the following decades the name slowly changed form and the city became widely known as Allahabad, a form commonly linked with the reign of Shah Jahan. Under British rule the spelling and the name stayed the same, and Allahabad became famous in India and abroad as a centre of administration, justice and learning.

In October 2018 the Uttar Pradesh government announced that the city would again be called Prayagraj, restoring the ancient name with the Hindi word for king, raj. You will still hear the older name in everyday speech, on old buildings and in the names of institutions such as the Allahabad High Court, the University of Allahabad and the Allahabad Museum. Both names are therefore useful when searching for places, trains and books.

The land, the rivers and the climate

Prayagraj sits on a flat, fertile plain a little under a hundred metres above sea level. The Ganga arrives from the north-west and the Yamuna from the south-west, and the city spreads across the land around their meeting point. The Yamuna, which has flowed down from the Himalayas through Delhi and Agra, is darker and bluish-green. The Ganga is lighter and silvery in colour. At the Sangam you can often see the two colours running side by side for a short distance before they blend into one broad river that continues east towards Varanasi.

The city has a humid subtropical climate with three clear seasons. Summers, from April to June, are very hot, with daytime temperatures often above 40 degrees Celsius and hot dry winds called loo. The monsoon arrives in July and lasts until about September. It brings heavy rain, and both rivers swell, sometimes flooding the low ghats and the Sangam shoreline. Winters, from November to February, are cool and pleasant in the day, but nights can be cold and foggy, especially in December and January.

This pattern matters for travellers. The pleasant months of October to March are also the season of the Magh Mela and, in special years, the Kumbh Mela. The rivers draw back after the monsoon and leave a wide sandy floodplain, and on this temporary land a huge tent city is built every year. Visiting in summer is possible but tiring, and visiting in the middle of the monsoon can mean closed ghats and muddy roads.

Ancient Prayag: the sacred beginnings

No one can say exactly when people first gathered at the confluence, but the area has been settled for a very long time. Archaeologists have found traces of early farming and village life in the Ganga plain around Prayagraj, and the surrounding region was part of the cradle of early Indian civilisation. Prayag is praised in the Vedic and Puranic literature as a place where bathing and giving charity bring great merit, and the Matsya Purana and other texts speak of it at length as the most sacred of all tirthas, or crossing places between the human and the divine.

The epics link the city to their stories. In the Ramayana, Rama, Sita and Lakshmana reach Prayag during their exile and visit the hermitage of the sage Bharadwaj before crossing the Yamuna. A few dozen kilometres away is Shringverpur, where the Nishad king Guha is said to have ferried Rama across the Ganga. The Mahabharata lists Prayag among the holy places visited by pilgrims, and describes the merit of living here through the month of Magha, a belief that still brings thousands of devotees to camp on the riverbank every winter.

Near Prayagraj, the ancient town of Kaushambi was the capital of the Vatsa kingdom, one of the great states of early India in the time of the Buddha. The Buddha is said to have taught there, and the remains of a large fortified city, monasteries and an Ashokan pillar have been found. In this period the whole region was a crossroads of trade routes and religions, with Vedic tradition, Buddhism and Jainism all present, and Prayag was already a famous pilgrim destination.

The story of the sacred confluence is also tied to the myth of the Kumbh. According to tradition, gods and demons churned the cosmic ocean to win the pot of immortal nectar, and as the pot was carried away, drops fell at four places: Prayag, Haridwar, Ujjain and Nashik. This story is the foundation of the Kumbh Mela, and Prayag's share of it, with the meeting of three rivers, is considered the most powerful of the four.

Maurya, Gupta and Harsha: pillars and charity

The best surviving proof of Prayag's importance in the early empires is a tall sandstone pillar, now inside the fort. It carries edicts of the Mauryan emperor Ashoka from the third century BCE, in which he speaks about compassion, tolerance and good government. Scholars believe it was originally set up at Kaushambi and moved to Prayag at a later date. The same column carries other inscriptions that make it a record of many centuries in one object.

The most famous of these is a long Sanskrit poem in praise of the Gupta emperor Samudragupta, written in the fourth century CE by his court poet Harisena. It lists his conquests, his generosity and his skills as a poet and musician, and it is one of the most important sources for Gupta history. Centuries later the Mughal emperor Jahangir added his own inscription, recording his accession to the throne. A single pillar thus holds the voices of a Mauryan emperor, a Gupta king and a Mughal ruler.

In the seventh century, Prayag appears in the accounts of the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang, also written Hiuen Tsang, who visited around 643 CE. He described the great assembly held by King Harshavardhana of Kannauj on the sandy plain at the confluence. Every five years Harsha distributed his accumulated treasure to the poor, to monks and to scholars of many faiths, until he had given away almost everything. It is one of the earliest descriptions of a large gathering on the very ground where the Kumbh and Magh Melas are held today.

The Mughal era: Akbar's fort and Illahabas

After the Delhi Sultanate, Prayag came under Mughal control. The emperor Akbar understood the value of the site. It lay where two great rivers met, close to the roads between Delhi, Bengal and central India, and it controlled river traffic across the plain. Between about 1575 and 1583 he built a massive fort of red sandstone on the bank of the Yamuna, with high walls, three grand gateways and rows of pillared halls. The settlement around it was called Illahabas, and the city grew as a military and administrative centre.

The fort was also a symbol. Akbar was known for his interest in many religions, and by placing his fortress at the most sacred meeting point of Hindu tradition he showed that his empire welcomed all faiths. Pilgrims continued to bathe at the confluence, and temples such as the underground Patalpuri shrine were allowed to remain under the fort. The Akshayavat, the undying banyan, has been described by travellers for many centuries.

The city also played a part in Mughal family politics. Prince Salim, the future emperor Jahangir, set up a rival court at Allahabad around 1599 during a period of tension with his father Akbar. After he became emperor, his son Prince Khusrau rose against him and was imprisoned. The lovely walled garden known as Khusro Bagh holds the tombs of Jahangir's first wife Shah Begum, her son Khusrau and his sister Nithar Begum. It is among the finest Mughal garden tombs in northern India and a quiet reminder of these royal dramas.

Later Mughal weakness in the eighteenth century left Allahabad exposed. Control passed between Mughal governors, regional powers and the nawabs of Awadh. In 1765, after the Battle of Buxar, the Treaty of Allahabad was signed in the fort, in which the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II granted the East India Company the right to collect revenue in Bengal, Bihar and Odisha. This agreement is often seen as a turning point in the rise of British power in India, and it happened inside the fort at Prayagraj.

The British period and the revolt of 1857

In 1801 the nawab of Awadh handed Allahabad to the East India Company by treaty, and the city became part of British territory. The Company valued the fort and the river crossing, and soon built barracks, courts and offices. The city's administrative importance grew steadily, and a new quarter called Civil Lines was laid out with wide roads, bungalows, churches and gardens, in the style of a planned colonial station.
